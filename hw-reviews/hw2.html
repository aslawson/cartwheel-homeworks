Giving the assistant a front door and a memory

The assistant already worked. This time I put it behind a proper entrance that checks who you are, and made every conversation leave a permanent record — so that later I can study thousands of them instead of reading one at a time.

Part one

What I did

  1. 1 Built the front door. You say who you are, it checks that claim against the user records, and hands back a pass that can't be edited. Your role and your shop are taken from the records — never from what you typed.
  2. 2 Made every message run inside a recording. Each conversation now leaves behind a labelled trail: who asked, which version of the instructions was in force, the question, and the answer.
  3. 3 Tagged every lookup with who triggered it — and whether it was allowed or refused. That turns "someone got refused somewhere" into something countable.
  4. 4 Tested it, ran it, and read the results. Eight tests on the parts that are genuinely enforced, seven real conversations through the new entrance, then the same question twice under two different versions of the instructions to prove the labels work.
Part two

What I learned

The idea underneath everything

You can't study what you didn't write down

All of this exists so that in a month I can ask "how often does it refuse someone who should have been allowed?" and get an answer from records rather than from memory. The catch is that recording isn't automatic and isn't free — something has to decide what's kept, at what level of detail, and labelled with what. Those decisions are the actual work.

The moment it clicked

The front door takes my word for who I am

I asked whether the code really checks identity, and the honest answer is that it doesn't. You say "I'm customer number 1" and it issues you a pass for customer number 1. There's no password anywhere.

Two questions I'd been treating as one thing, and they aren't:

Never checked

Are you who you say you are? Nothing verifies it. In a real product this would sit behind a login.

Strictly enforced

Given who you are, what may you do? Role and shop come from the records, never from the conversation.

Separating them also explained a rule I'd been told and only half understood: the pass is issued once, at the door, and nothing said inside the conversation can change it. Otherwise a customer could simply announce a promotion mid-sentence. I wrote tests for the half that's real — including one that edits a pass to claim a different identity and confirms it gets rejected.

On what to record

Record at the level you'll actually want to read

A lot of recording comes free. The problem is that free recording captures what the software thinks matters — every round of thinking, with the full history and every lookup result attached, nested several layers deep. I went looking for the assistant's final answer in one and couldn't find it: buried under three collapsed layers, split into pieces.

The question asked and the answer given had to be recorded deliberately, as their own thing.

Which was the other surprise: the recording also keeps the assistant's private reasoning, the part the customer never sees. Useful for understanding a decision, and a real privacy question, because it can contain more than the reply does. There's a switch to turn content capture off, and now I know what it's for.

On comparing anything

Stamp every recording with a version of the instructions

The assistant's behaviour depends on the wording it was given, and that wording changes. So every recording carries a short code identifying the exact version in force at the time. I tested it properly: same question, same customer, same data, only the instructions different.

Original wording · d919fdad59ca

"You're still within the 30-day return window per Cartwheel's return policy."

After my edit · 7554069158a9

"You're still within the 30-day return window (per policy cw-returns)."

Without those codes, a month of recordings is one undifferentiated pile and every comparison inside it is meaningless.

The thing I nearly overclaimed

Proving the plumbing works isn't proving the change helped

Those two answers are, for practical purposes, identical. It would be easy to write that up as "the edit made no difference" — and that would be wrong in the other direction.

Two reasons it proves nothing either way. The question I used doesn't exercise what the edit was about. And it's one run against one run: the assistant words things differently every time, so a difference this small is indistinguishable from it simply having a different morning.

What this did establish is narrower and worth having: the labelling works. Measuring whether a change is an improvement takes many runs and a comparison of rates — which is, conveniently, what comes next in the course.

Small things, learned the hard way

Four practical truths

Restart after editing instructions. The wording is read once when the program starts. Edit the file on a running program and it carries on with the old version, cheerfully, with no error to warn you.

Two things called "session" aren't the same thing. Who you are lives in memory and vanishes on restart. What was said lives on disk and survives. Once I saw that, a confusing error message became obvious.

Expect port collisions. The recording tools wanted two ports my work setup already had. One line each to move — but only after working out which number was mine to change and which belonged to the software.

A "not found" in a log isn't a fault. Mine was a browser poking at the server looking for something that was never there.

Where it ended up. A front door with identity checks, a recording of every request, eight tests covering the parts that are genuinely enforced, seven conversations across all three kinds of user, and a controlled comparison of two versions of the instructions.
