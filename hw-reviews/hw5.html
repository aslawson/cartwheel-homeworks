Teaching a grader one mistake

Homework four named the mistakes by hand. This week I picked one — the assistant sending customers somewhere else, or making up a process, when they ask for something it can't do — and built an AI grader to spot it. Then I checked how often the grader agrees with me on conversations it had never seen.

Part one

What I did

  1. 1 Picked one fault and hunted for examples of it. Homework four left me 8 failures and 23 clean cases. I needed 30 of each, so I read the conversations most likely to go wrong: questions about things the shop can't do, like account changes, card disputes, the weather, and legal advice.
  2. 2 Wrote the rule down — what counts as sending someone elsewhere, what counts as a made-up process, and what is a different mistake that this grader should ignore.
  3. 3 Split my answers three ways before writing anything. Fifteen conversations to use as examples in the grader's instructions, 28 for practice, and 28 locked away for one final exam.
  4. 4 Wrote the grader's instructions and practised. The first version disagreed with me on 11 of 28 practice conversations. I read every disagreement, fixed the instructions once, and the second version agreed with me on 20 of 28.
  5. 5 Locked the second version and sat the exam once. It passed the good replies well and caught fewer than half of the real failures.
Part two

What I learned

Where the time actually went

Finding the failures was the work, not building the grader

The grader can't be tested without enough real failures to test it against, and a well-behaved assistant mostly behaves. Homework four had given me eight. Where the other twenty came from:

  • 13 of 27Questions the shop can't serve — account changes, disputes, off-topic asks. About one in two.
  • 3 of 9Conversations that merely looked like my earlier failures. Similar wording, mostly fine behaviour, and slow going.
  • 4 of 4Replies flagged by a scan for phrases like “the carrier,” “your bank,” or “your dashboard.” Thirteen flagged, five worth reading, four failures from the four I labelled. A cheap scan, and the best hit rate of the three.

I stopped at 28 failures against a target of 30.

The question I kept asking

“Did it make that up?” has a checkable answer

Half my labelling time went on one question: is this instruction real? The way to settle it is the shop's own policy pages, not my sense of what sounds normal.

Sounds made up, is real

“Change your card in account settings.” “Payouts run on Fridays.” “Tracking is on your order page.” All three are written policy.

Sounds normal, is invented

“Contact the store through its page.” “Refuse the delivery.” “Five to seven business days.” No policy says any of these.

The invented ones are the dangerous kind. They are exactly what a reasonable person would expect a shop to say, so they don't look wrong.

The rule grew while I used it

My own labels knew a rule I hadn't written

When I picked examples for the grader, two of my “failure” labels didn't fit my written rule. In both, a customer's dispute was past the 60-day limit, and the assistant offered to pass it to a person who “may review it case by case.” Nobody was sent elsewhere and nothing was invented — yet I'd failed both.

Hinting at a way round a policy limit is a made-up process too. It just doesn't name one.

That went into the rule. A second line went in after the first trial run: saying a refund's timing “depends on your bank” is information, not a redirect. It only fails when the customer is told to go and deal with someone else.

The disagreement that taught the most

The first grader marked down an assistant for doing its job

On practice, the first version of the grader failed eight replies I'd called fine. The clearest pattern among them: it decided that ordinary help was overreach.

First grader said

“Would you like me to go ahead and issue the refund now?” is an invented procedure. Fail.

What's true

Issuing refunds is one of the assistant's own tools, and refunds of $100 or less go through automatically. The grader was wrong.

It did the same to a shop owner looking up their own listings, and to an eligible refund for a parcel that never arrived. The fix was to tell it what the assistant is allowed to do, and that ordinary requests the shop can serve are not its business. That one revision took agreement from 17 of 28 to 20 of 28. I stopped there, time-boxed.

Why the exam is kept locked

Practice flattered the grader

On practice, the second version caught 7 of 11 failures. On the exam — 28 conversations it had never seen, run exactly once — it caught 5 of 11. It still passed good replies reliably: 14 of 17.

That gap is the whole reason for keeping a locked set. Every fix I made was a fix for the practice conversations, so practice scores drift upward whether or not the grader got better in general. The exam is the only number that wasn't steered.

And eleven failures is not many. The honest statement is that this grader catches somewhere between one in five and seven in ten of these failures. That range is wide enough that more labels would matter more than more prompt tuning.

Vocabulary that tripped me up

TPR and TNR aren't precision and recall

In this course “fine” is the positive class. So the true positive rate is the share of my “fine” labels the grader also passed, which is recall. The true negative rate is the share of my failures it caught, which is recall for the other side. Precision is a different question — when the grader says something, how often is it right — and it shifts depending on how common failures are. The two rates don't, which is what lets a later exercise correct a measured failure rate for the grader's own mistakes.

Plumbing, briefly

Small substitutions, written down

The assignment specifies an OpenAI model; I only have an Anthropic key, so the grader ran on Claude Haiku 4.5 for both practice and the exam. The course's helper only recognised Google and OpenAI keys, so that check was widened, and the offline test that guarantees no accidental paid calls now clears the Anthropic key too. One reply came back as “Pass” with stray line breaks, so the verdict check now ignores surrounding whitespace — and still rejects anything that isn't Pass or Fail. The whole thing cost well under a dollar.

Where it ended up. 71 labelled conversations (43 fine, 28 failures), a written rule, a split made once, two versions of the grader with every practice disagreement on file, and one locked exam: 82% agreement on fine replies, 45% of failures caught, with wide ranges on both.
